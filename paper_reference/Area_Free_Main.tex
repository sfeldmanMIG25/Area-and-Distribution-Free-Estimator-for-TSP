\begin{abstract}
We present the Geometrically Assisted Regression Tree (GART) 2.0, an improved estimator, to demonstrate that a learned model can give a reliable tour-length estimate with bounded complexity across dimensions and distributions. Existing estimators either assume a known sampling density or depend on planar geometry that becomes unstable as dimension grows. GART 2.0 is a LightGBM regressor that predicts the instance-specific ratio of tour length to minimum-spanning-tree length. This iteration improves on the initial estimator by avoiding features that scale poorly in high dimensions. Trained on synthetic instances alone, it transfers to TSPLIB95 and, through a multidimensional-scaling embedding, to non-Euclidean instances. GART 2.0 compared favorably to 7 other estimators across 4 distinct benchmarks. Comparison against the known Held--Karp 1-tree lower bound, demonstrates the GART 2.0 approaches the frontier of prediction quality and performance, especially in large cases. The reason is structural: the bound pays a quadratic price in instance size for a fixed accuracy in every dimension, while the estimator's cost carries no accuracy term---so the estimator sharpens exactly where an exact solve becomes infeasible. Comparing GART 2.0's performance to classical estimators and bounds shows it has a niche in high-throughput tour-length estimation for large instances. However, it requires further refinement to perform better on non-Euclidean real-world data.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

The Traveling Salesman Problem (TSP) is NP-hard \citep{karp1972, papadimitriou1977} and appears throughout logistics operations planning, network design, and resource allocation \citep{chien1992operational, concorde2006}. It often enters as a subproblem of a larger optimization, where only its cost is needed. A facility-location decision, for example, requires the route cost of every candidate, so a full solve is paid once per candidate.

\subsection{Background and Motivation}

Last-mile delivery accounts for approximately 53\% of total shipping costs by one industry estimate \citep{finmile2025}, and the UPS ORION system is reported to save roughly 100 million miles and \$300--400 million annually through route optimization \citep{holland2017ups}. In such systems orders arrive continuously and routes are long, so the cost of a full solve is paid many times over. An estimator replaces that solve wherever only the relative cost of candidate routes matters.

The same estimation problem recurs wherever a TSP appears as a subproblem, and it rarely arrives in the plane. Radiation hybrid mapping orders genetic markers by a TSP over an abstract similarity metric \citep{agarwala2000fast}. Mobile-sink routing in wireless sensor networks contains a TSP through the data-collection locations \citep{yuan2010racetrack}. UAV structural inspection places viewpoints around a three-dimensional structure and solves a TSP through them \citep{bircher2016threedimensional}. Large-scale routing methods partition an instance into sub-TSPs and compare candidate partitions \citep{frontiers2021}, and \citet{aaai2023} learn a hierarchical policy that repeatedly selects node subsets and routes through them. Each of these planners needs the cheaper option predicted as cheaper, so the estimator must be accurate and rank-preserving.

 Most classical tour-length estimators, however, are built for planar Euclidean inputs and become unstable or intractable as dimension grows \citep{chazelle1993}. An estimator for these settings must be defined for arbitrary $d$ without using features that scale poorly. A distance matrix admits a Euclidean representation of bounded distortion once enough dimensions are allowed \citep{bourgain1985}, so an estimator that stays accurate at those dimensions can consume the embedding.

\subsection{Limitations of the Current Estimators} \label{subsec:related}
No published estimator reaches high-dimensional or non-Euclidean instances without losing calibration or requiring an embedding. The Held--Karp 1-tree bound reaches both directly, but it is a known bound, rather than a classical estimator. The Beardwood--Halton--Hammersley (BHH) theorem \citep{bhh1959} is stated for arbitrary dimension, but it is asymptotic in $n$ and its calibrating constants and density integral are not recoverable from an arbitrary finite point set. Published constants exist for $d=2$ \citep{johnson1996asymptotic} and $d=3$ \citep{percus1996finite}, and above that only a large-$d$ approximation \citep{bertsimas1990asymptotic}. The estimators of \citet{chien1992operational} are planar and depend on particular depot and covering-region definitions. A parallel line of work is tuned to the 2D Euclidean case: \citet{daganzo1984b} gives a strip-based construction, \citet{kwon1995tsp} fit regression and neural models on rectangular-region predictors, and \citet{cavdar2015distribution} propose a distribution-free estimator built from per-axis spread statistics about the covering rectangle. Space-filling heuristics such as the planar construction of \citet{bartholdi1982heuristic} instead build a tour outright in $O(n\log n)$. Embedding a non-Euclidean instance therefore returns the classical estimators above to the dimensions where their geometric inputs are unstable. 

Recent neural approaches address the same gap from the learning side. \citet{varol2023neural} report sub-1\% deviation on standard distributions by combining a multilayer perceptron (MLP) with domain-knowledge features, but their pipeline requires solution-level features, which are computationally expensive and challenging to generalize across all instance sizes. \citet{kou2022standard} use the standard deviation of random feasible tour lengths as a single-feature, distribution-independent predictor on Euclidean and non-Euclidean instances, but the repeated tour sampling raises its per-instance cost. \citet{kou2024randomization} extend this principle beyond the TSP to the vehicle-routing (VRP), MST, and matching problems, again from the standard deviation of random feasible solutions. We exclude that sampling cost on the same grounds as solution-level features. The closest methodological neighbor is \citet{kou2023sammon}, which pairs a Sammon-map embedding with a distribution-independent estimator on non-Euclidean TSPLIB95 instances. Our pipeline uses classical metric MDS for the embedding and computes MST-topology features on the original distance matrix, not on the embedded coordinates.

 \citet{heldkarp1970,heldkarp1971} relax the Hamiltonian-cycle constraint to a \emph{1-tree}, a spanning tree on all but one node plus any two edges at that node. The minimum 1-tree under any vector of node penalties yields a proven lower bound on the optimal tour. The bound uses no training data and no distributional assumption, and it does not invoke the triangle inequality: it is defined for any symmetric distance matrix, including the non-Euclidean instances Section~\ref{sec:application} reaches only through an embedding. Its cost is quadratic in $n$ per ascent iteration.  In the plane and at $d=3$, Section~\ref{sec:frontier} shows that GART 2.0 beats this bound at large $n$: feature extraction is subquadratic while the ascent is quadratic in every dimension, and the accuracy it gives up is small.

\subsection{Contribution}
We present the Geometrically Assisted Regression Tree (GART) 2.0, a LightGBM regressor \citep{ke2017lightgbm} that predicts a per-instance scaling factor $\hat{\alpha}$ rather than applying a fixed constant. It is distribution-independent and supports arbitrary dimensions: all 31 features are defined for arbitrary $d$ through MST topology, centroid dispersion and coordinate ranges. GART 2.0 gives stronger accuracy and precision than classical tour-length estimators across a broad range of cases, at higher computational cost. However, compared with the known lower bound, it is the more efficient instrument on large instances (Section~\ref{sec:frontier}). 





\section{Theoretical Foundations} \label{sec:theory}

The ratio $\alpha=L_{\mathrm{TSP}}/L_{\mathrm{MST}}$ varies from instance to instance. Let $\mathcal{G}=(V,E)$ be a complete undirected graph on the node set $V=\{1,\dots,n\}$ with non-negative edge costs $c_{ij}$, typically the Euclidean distance between points in $\mathbb{R}^d$. Throughout, $n$ is the number of nodes and $d$ is the dimension of the coordinate space. The TSP asks for a minimum-cost Hamiltonian cycle, a connected 2-regular spanning subgraph \citep{dantzig1954solution,concorde2006}. Relaxing 2-regularity to connectivity alone gives the minimum spanning tree (MST).

Prim's algorithm constructs one in $O(n^2)$ on a complete graph \citep{prim1957}, and in $\mathbb{R}^d$ the pairwise distances cost $O(n^2 d)$, linear in $d$. Removing the heaviest edge from any tour leaves a spanning tree, so $L_{\mathrm{MST}} \le L_{\mathrm{TSP}}$. The double-tree heuristic \citep{rosenkrantz1977} builds a feasible tour of length at most $2\,L_{\mathrm{MST}}$ on any instance obeying the triangle inequality. Together these give
\begin{equation} \label{eq:bounds}
    L_{\mathrm{MST}} \le L_{\mathrm{TSP}} \le 2 \cdot L_{\mathrm{MST}}.
\end{equation}
For collinear point sets the optimal tour must traverse the line to its far end and return, so $L_{\mathrm{TSP}} = 2\,L_{\mathrm{MST}}$ exactly.

A tighter known lower bound comes from the Held--Karp relaxation. Fix a node $s$. A \emph{1-tree} is a spanning tree on $V\setminus\{s\}$ together with any two edges incident to $s$. Every Hamiltonian tour is a 1-tree, so the minimum 1-tree relaxes the tour problem. Introduce node potentials $\pi\in\mathbb{R}^{n}$ and transformed costs $c^{\pi}_{ij}=c_{ij}+\pi_i+\pi_j$. Under $c^{\pi}$ every tour's cost shifts by exactly $2\sum_i\pi_i$, because every node of a tour has degree two, and for \emph{any} real $\pi$
\begin{equation} \label{eq:hk}
    w(\pi) \;=\; L_{\mathrm{1tree}}(\pi) \;-\; 2\sum_{i\in V}\pi_i \;\le\; L_{\mathrm{TSP}},
\end{equation}
which is the Held--Karp bound \citep{heldkarp1970,heldkarp1971}. The map $w$ is concave and piecewise linear, a subgradient at $\pi$ is $g_i=\deg_i(\pi)-2$, and subgradient ascent over $\pi$ approaches $\max_\pi w(\pi)$. Each iteration builds its spanning tree on the perturbed costs directly. The ascent is not monotone in $w$, so the scored quantity at budget $k$ is the incumbent $\hat w_k=\max_{j\le k}w(\pi_j)$. Eq.~\eqref{eq:hk} needs neither $\pi\ge0$ nor $c^{\pi}\ge0$, so $\hat w_k$ is a valid bound at any budget.

The Christofides--Serdyukov algorithm \citep{christofides1976, serdyukov1978} returns a tour within a factor $\tfrac{3}{2}$ of the \emph{optimum}, and \citet{karlin2021slightly} reach a randomized $\tfrac{3}{2}-\epsilon$ approximation by a different construction. Both bound an algorithm's output against the optimal tour, so neither tightens the intrinsic ratio $L_{\mathrm{TSP}}/L_{\mathrm{MST}}$, which can still equal $2$. The matching step costs $O(n^3)$ \citep{edmonds1965,gabow1990,cook1999} against $O(n^2)$ for the MST, so the Christofides refinement is impractical as an estimation subroutine for increasing $n$.

For symmetric metric instances we therefore treat $\alpha \in [1, 2]$ as an intrinsic, instance-dependent quantity to be predicted. Over our training split the standard deviation of $\alpha$ falls monotonically from 0.184 at $d=2$ to 0.0762 at $d=50$, while the cost of computing either quantity does not. For uniformly distributed points the TSP constant itself varies with dimension \citep{percus1996finite}, and no published MST constant covers every $d$. A constant calibrated in the plane is therefore biased at higher $d$. GART 2.0 predicts $\alpha$ from the observed instance features instead.

\section{Methodology: The Geometrically Assisted Regression Tree} \label{sec:methodology}

GART 2.0 extends the estimator of \citet{gart2025}. It predicts $\alpha$ and returns $\hat{L}_{\mathrm{TSP}}=\hat{\alpha}L_{\mathrm{MST}}$. We constrain the regression target to $\alpha\in[1,2]$, the metric-TSP interval implied by Eq.~\eqref{eq:bounds}.

\subsection{Feature Selection} \label{subsec:features}



\subsubsection{Geometric and Centroid Descriptors}

The geometric descriptors, such as bounding hypervolume and centroid distance, generalize to arbitrary $d$ without a convex hull. They encode the scale information of the BHH volume term \citep{bhh1959} and adapt centroid statistics from \citet{cavdar2015distribution}. Before evaluating them we rotate the coordinates into their principal-axis frame \citep{jolliffe2002}, which makes the axis-aligned bounding-box descriptors independent of the input orientation. The rotation costs $O(nd^2+d^3)$ and is dominated by dense MST construction whenever $n$ is large relative to $d$. Appendix~\ref{app:features} lists the geometric group.

\subsubsection{MST Edge and Topological Features}

MST edge weights and the degree sequence are invariant to rotation, translation and coordinate-system choice. The MST's total length is not among the edge and topology statistics, because it is the multiplicand $L_{\mathrm{MST}}$ and withholding it keeps the learned quantity a scale-free ratio. The one input GART 2.0 adds to the 30-feature block it inherits is \texttt{greedy\_nn\_over\_mst}, the length of an exact greedy nearest-neighbor tour divided by $L_{\mathrm{MST}}$. A greedy tour is feasible, so its length is at least $L_{\mathrm{TSP}}$ and the ratio is a constructed upper bound on $\alpha$. That ratio also gates coverage on the non-Euclidean path of Section~\ref{sec:application}, where the metric is given but coordinates are not. The estimator returns a status row instead of a prediction for any instance whose ratio falls outside $[1.035,2.209]$, the range the ratio spans over the full 106{,}272-row corpus, training, validation and test together. The training split alone spans the narrower $[1.0465,2.1295]$.

\subsection{Dataset Generation} \label{subsec:dataset}

We built the corpus from four one-dimensional generators: Uniform, Normal, Clustered, and Correlated (Appendix~\ref{app:training}). Each axis of each instance draws its generator independently, so configurations are anisotropic and topology varies by axis. Over all $2{,}444{,}256$ axes, 68.7\% are uniform, 12.5\% clustered, 12.5\% normal, and 6.2\% correlated. The multidimensional results below should be read against that mix.

The full feature corpus comprises $106{,}272$ solved instances with node counts $n \in [5, 1000]$ and dimensions $d \in \{2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 10, 15, 20, 25, 30, 35, 40, 45, 50\}$ (17 training-visible dimension values), plus a held-out extrapolation bucket at $d = 100$. Instances are partitioned into training ($69{,}768$), validation ($19{,}584$), and test ($16{,}920$) via a stratified split on $(d, n, \text{grid size})$; all $d = 100$ instances are locked into the test partition. For each instance, the generation pipeline ran Concorde \citep{concorde2006} and LKH-3 \citep{helsgaun2017} and retained the shorter tour. 

\subsection{Model Architecture and Training} \label{subsec:model_training}

The estimator for $\alpha$ is a gradient-boosted decision tree (GBDT) ensemble \citep{friedman2001greedy}, implemented in LightGBM \citep{ke2017lightgbm}. The learner uses squared-error regression on the logit-transformed target $z=\log\!\big((\alpha-1)/(2-\alpha)\big)$, and predictions are returned through its inverse $\alpha=1+(1+e^{-z})^{-1}$. The bound $\alpha\in[1,2]$ is therefore structural. The inverse transform cannot leave the open interval $(1,2)$, so the collinear extreme of Eq.~\eqref{eq:bounds} is never attained. A clip to $[1,2]$ remains in the released wrapper as a guard against non-finite inputs, and it is never binding. On the 16{,}920 multidimensional test predictions $\hat\alpha$ fell in $[1.03,1.92]$. The hyperparameters are inherited and frozen. An Optuna search \citep{akiba2019optuna} against in-distribution validation moved multidimensional MAPE by 0.011 percentage points while costing 0.16 points of TSPLIB MAPE, and we rejected it (Appendix~\ref{app:hyperparams}). 


\subsection{Feature Importance}\label{subsec:shap}

By mean absolute SHAP magnitude \citep{lundberg2017shap} against the released booster, the MST dominance ratio contributes 26.5\% of the total and \texttt{greedy\_nn\_over\_mst} 23.9\%. Node count and dimension jointly contribute 14.1\%. Appendix~\ref{app:shap} gives the group-level shares and the sample they are measured on.

A \emph{cell} is one exact dimension and one size band of the held-out test split. Within cells the greedy-to-MST ratio holds 35.0\% of the SHAP variance at $d=2$, flat across every band, and the MST dominance ratio holds 23.9\% to 27.5\%. Within-cell fit in $\alpha$ space rises with dimension, from $R^2$ 0.870 at $d=2$ to 0.974 at the withheld $d=100$. The generator mix explains 14.1\% of the within-cell target at $d=2$ and close to zero by $d=100$. The geometric group carries scale. The MST edge statistics track the sampling distribution, and that dependence fades with dimension.

\section{Benchmarking} \label{sec:evaluation}

Each results table reports the standard deviation of percent error (SDPE), the mean absolute percentage error (MAPE), the median absolute percentage error (the $|$Median$|$ column), and the mean signed percent error (MSPE). Rows that predict a multiple of $L_{\mathrm{MST}}$ also carry $R^2_\alpha=1-\sum_i(\hat\alpha_i-\alpha_i)^2/\sum_i(\alpha_i-\bar\alpha)^2$, with $\bar\alpha$ the mean of $\alpha$ over the reported bucket. SDPE is the primary metric:
\[
\text{SDPE} = 100 \cdot \sqrt{\frac{1}{N-1}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left(e_i - \bar e\right)^2}, \qquad \bar e = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N} e_i.
\]
Here $e_i=(\hat L_i-L_i)/L_i$ is the signed relative error and $N$ is the number of scored instances.
 Several baselines beat it in individual cells. Each SDPE carries a 95\%  confidence interval. The intervals are computed per estimator and ignore the pairing, so overlapping intervals establish nothing on their own. We test each comparison on the instances where both estimators produce a finite prediction, using the paired difference in absolute percent error with a 1{,}000-resample paired bootstrap and a Wilcoxon signed-rank test.

\subsection{Benchmark Roster} \label{subsec:bench_models}



We score estimators against their published domains. In the BHH form, $\mathcal{V}$ is the measure of the sampling region \citep{bhh1959}: $\beta_2=0.7124$ \citep{johnson1996asymptotic}, $\beta_3=0.6979$ \citep{percus1996finite}, and the conjectured large-$d$ asymptotic is $\beta_d\approx\sqrt{d/(2\pi e)}$ for $d\ge4$ \citep{bertsimas1990asymptotic}. In the \c{C}avdar--Sokol form, $A$ is the area of the rectangle covering the nodes, $\mathrm{stdev}_j$ is the standard deviation of the raw coordinates on axis $j$, and $\bar c_j$ and $\mathrm{cstdev}_j$ are the mean and standard deviation of the absolute distances from the nodes to that rectangle's midpoint line on axis $j$. The \c{C}avdar--Sokol estimate is divided by the finite-$n$ adjustment factor its source fits, held fixed outside the node range that fit covers. Both constant sets and the adjustment factor come from \citet{cavdar2014dissertation}. The asymptotic ratio's constant, $\beta_{\mathrm{TSP}}/\beta_{\mathrm{MST}}\approx1.1253$ \citep{johnson1996asymptotic,cortinaborja2000mst}, comes from the literature; $\hat\rho(d,n)$ is fitted on the training split.



GART 1.0 \citep{gart2025} is our prior model and the one learned estimator in the baseline count. Originally trained for the VRP, it takes the first unique coordinate as the depot for two features. GART 2.0 also has a more diverse and larger training, features, and training, so the comparison isolates no single causal change. The planar guarantee of \citet{bartholdi1982heuristic} does not transfer to the Hilbert row's multidimensional variant (Table~\ref{tab:benchmark_models}).

On the full EUC\_2D benchmark GART 2.0 obtains 2.53\% MAPE and 2.92\% SDPE, against 3.53\%/4.74\% for the asymptotic ratio. This is the narrowest margin in the paper. $\alpha$ on these 78 instances has mean 1.13 and standard deviation 0.0558, so a single well-chosen constant is already close. The MAPE-minimizing constant on these instances is $1.13$ and reaches 3.52\%, so no constant multiplier does better on MAPE, and GART 2.0's 2.53\% improves on that bound by 0.99 percentage points. The dispersion gap is the larger one, 2.92\% SDPE against 4.74\%, but SDPE alone cannot carry that comparison: for $\hat L=cL_{\mathrm{MST}}$ the signed error is $c/\alpha-1$, so SDPE is proportional to $c$ and shrinking $c$ drives it to zero at ruinous MAPE. GART 2.0 attains the lower SDPE at the lower MAPE. The largest instance, \texttt{d18512}, is more than $18\times$ the training-size cap.

Both classical estimators are far behind here. TSPLIB instances carry no uniform density on a known region, so each area-based estimator runs outside the conditions it was derived under. BHH receives a convex hull in place of a sampling region that does not exist. \c{C}avdar--Sokol is evaluated on graphs without a node at the corners of their covering rectangle, a property every one of its training graphs had. Both overpredict. These rows measure the cost of that mismatch, not the quality of the cited methods.

\subsection{Matched-Domain Comparison} \label{subsec:matched_domain}

Estimators marketed across domains do better on these generalized benchmarks than others.

BHH falls from 23.8\% MAPE on the full 2D benchmark to 8.91\% here, and \c{C}avdar--Sokol from 18.2\% to 8.16\%. \c{C}avdar--Sokol itself did not change between panels, so its drop is a domain effect. BHH's region input changed with the instance set, so its drop combines the two effects and does not isolate the region input. What remains of BHH's error is a systematic underprediction, $-8.65$\% signed against 7.76\% SDPE, the direction an asymptotic constant takes at $n$ as low as 5. \c{C}avdar--Sokol carries the lower MAPE of the two on every corpus and the lower median absolute error here, 2.84\%, at the widest dispersion in the panel, 14.3\% SDPE. It is close on most uniform instances and badly wrong on a few.

GART 2.0 beats both classical estimators on the matched domain, on both metrics. On the 210 uniform instances it obtains 1.31\% MAPE against \c{C}avdar--Sokol's 8.16\%, BHH's 8.91\% and the $\alpha=1$ floor's 15.6\%. Its SDPE is 2.07\% against \c{C}avdar--Sokol's 14.3\%, BHH's 7.76\% and the $\alpha=1$ floor's 7.70\%. 

\subsection{Generalization Beyond the Training Grid} \label{subsec:generalization}


Withholding both $d=15$ and $d=25$ from training, 11.8\% of the training rows, raises test MAPE at $d=15$ from 0.50\% to 0.58\% and at $d=25$ from 0.35\% to 0.38\%. The neighboring trained dimensions $d\in\{10,20,30\}$ do not degrade at all, moving from 0.43\% to 0.42\%, so the loss is specific to the withheld strata, not to the reduced training volume. It takes the form of a bias shift. Mean signed error at $d=15$ moves from $-0.008$\% to $+0.134$\%. Training only on $n\le200$ and testing on $n\in(200,1000]$ raises MAPE from 0.41\% to 0.59\%, again almost entirely as a uniform offset. SDPE moves only from 0.47\% to 0.56\% while mean signed error rises from $+0.28$\% to $+0.51$\%, a factor of 1.8.

The refits vary only the training data, at fixed hyperparameters and seed, and the baseline among them reproduces the released model bit-for-bit at 0.618\% MAPE over the 16{,}846 scored rows. Off the grid the model acquires a systematic offset and keeps its structure. We demonstrate no correction for that offset. 

\subsection{Failure on Near-Collinear Geometry} \label{subsec:coverage}

Section~\ref{subsec:results_2d} reports 10.8\% MAPE on the near-collinear Line Noise class against 1.47\% on the isotropic class, and the error is almost entirely one-sided: MSPE is $-10.8$, so the model under-predicts.

TreeSHAP on the released booster, run over the 2{,}580 diverse 2D benchmark instances, shows why. On the Isotropic class (840 instances) the Greedy-to-MST Ratio carries 28.7\% of the total mean $|\mathrm{SHAP}|$, the largest share of any feature; the MST Dominance Ratio is next at 19.3\%, then dimension at 8.16\%, $n$ at 7.97\%, and normalized MST diameter at 6.36\%. Isotropic predictions are unbiased: mean predicted $\alpha$ is 1.22 against a mean true $\alpha$ of 1.22.

On Line Noise (210 instances) its mean signed SHAP rises from $+0.417$ on Isotropic to $+0.732$ on Line Noise, and the MST Dominance Ratio's from $+0.105$ to $+0.369$, both correctly pushing $\alpha$ up. The prediction still falls short: mean predicted $\alpha$ is 1.38 against a mean true $\alpha$ of 1.56, a shortfall of 0.174, the largest of any class (the jittered-grid class is next at 0.0738). The training split itself contains zero instances with $\alpha>1.5$ at $n\ge200$; its $\alpha$ 99th percentile is 1.54, and 54\% of the 210 Line Noise instances exceed it.

The reason is support, on exactly the feature carrying the prediction. On the training split the Greedy-to-MST Ratio spans $[1.06,1.67]$ between its 1st and 99th percentiles; on Line Noise it averages 1.77, and 78.1\% of the class's rows fall outside that band. The other top features stay inside it: the MST Dominance Ratio is 14.3\% outside; dimension, $n$, and MST diameter are 0\% outside. A boosted tree cannot predict past the highest split value it saw in training, so the output saturates while the true $\alpha$ keeps rising.

\subsection{Rank Agreement and Calibration} \label{subsec:discussion}

The estimator must \emph{order} instances the way the stored references do even when its absolute numbers are off. Cross-instance cost varies over orders of magnitude and $L_{\mathrm{MST}}$ already tracks that variation, so global rank correlation is close to 1 for every estimator here and demonstrates nothing about the learned ratio. The close-pair statistic is a plain concordant fraction and sits at $0.5$ under an unrelated ordering. Both metrics are reported with $L_{\mathrm{MST}}$ itself as the control. Every constant multiplier is a monotone transform of $L_{\mathrm{MST}}$ and scores identically to it, so the asymptotic-ratio and $\alpha=1$ controls coincide on 2D and TSPLIB.

For pairs satisfying $|L_A-L_B|/\max(L_A,L_B)<5\%$, GART 2.0 orders 74.5\% of 74{,}826 2D pairs, 92.4\% of 1{,}647{,}719 multidimensional pairs, and 72.2\% of 54 TSPLIB pairs correctly, against 71.0\%, 69.1\%, and 61.1\% for the $\alpha=1$ control. The multidimensional gain of 23.4 percentage points is the substantive one. The 2D gain of 3.44 points is not, and 54 TSPLIB pairs cannot support an 11.1-point claim. At a 10\% threshold GART 2.0 obtains 83.6\%, 96.1\%, and 81.0\%, against 77.8\%, 75.9\%, and 73.3\% for the $\alpha=1$ control. The percentages remain descriptive because the pairs share instances and no block-bootstrap interval is computed over them. GART 1.0 trails GART 2.0 on the TSPLIB close-pair statistic at the tighter threshold, at 70.4\%.

The $n>400$ TSPLIB bucket has $R^2_\alpha=-0.0710$. The model predicts $\alpha$ there worse than the bucket mean does. That bucket has low target variance and an upward mean prediction offset while its tour-cost MAPE remains 2.90\%, so the cost-level result does not remove the ratio-calibration failure. On TSPLIB EUC\_2D the difference between GART 2.0 and the asymptotic MST ratio is $-0.997$ percentage points with a 95\% interval of $[-1.77,-0.224]$ and $p=0.0049$, so the MAPE advantage is small but significant.

\subsection{Computational Cost} \label{subsec:cost_accounting}

Feature extraction dominates the estimator's cost. GART 2.0 spends 92.9\% of its wall time on feature extraction and MST construction against 5.03\% on LightGBM inference. We time each instance as the median of 11 repeats, one estimator per process on a single thread. The Time column of Table~\ref{tab:tsplib_by_size} prints the median of those times across instances. Timed the same way, GART 2.0 costs roughly twice the MST-ratio family and an order of magnitude over the two classical closed forms, which compute a convex hull and then evaluate an algebraic expression. 

Those multiples are ratios of medians, not of summed totals, and are the wrong comparison for deployment, where the alternative is a solve. On the multidimensional benchmark the median prediction and the median reference-tour generation cost the same, 121~ms against 122~ms, since most of those instances are small enough to solve outright. The argument holds only where the solve is expensive: at $n\in[501,1000]$ the reference tour takes 3{,}799~ms against GART 2.0's 460~ms, and on TSPLIB \texttt{d18512}, at $n=18{,}512$ and more than $18\times$ the largest training instance, GART 2.0 predicts in 237~ms against the known lower bound, which costs 167 times as much at the crossing budget and 2{,}078 times as much, 493 seconds, at the largest ascent budget. 

\section{Comparison Against a Known Lower Bound} \label{sec:frontier}

 The Held--Karp 1-tree bound is cheaper and more accurate than GART 2.0 on aggregate in all benchmarks. Those averages, however, do not show how either family's cost and accuracy change as $n$ grows. 

The bound $\hat w_k$ is the best value the ascent reaches within a budget of $k$ iterations. It never exceeds the optimum, so its error against a reference tour is the room it leaves below the optimum plus whatever that reference sits above it. The \emph{calibrated} bound $c_k\,\hat w_k$ multiplies it by one constant, which we fit per budget on the planar training split and never on a scored instance. That constant lifts the bound to where the tour length sits and makes it comparable with a trained estimator. That row is not a lower bound, since $c_k>1$ pushes some predictions above the optimum, and it is not training-free. We fit the constant in the plane, so the multidimensional table reports the raw bound $\hat w_k$ alone.

Both ascents run on all four benchmarks, and each headline table reports the arm that leads on its own corpus. The planar tables use the Volgenant--Jonker step schedule with Helsgaun's direction smoothing \citep{volgenant1982,helsgaun2000}. The multidimensional tables use a Polyak step \citep{polyak1969} against a constructive upper bound, a nearest-neighbor tour improved by 2-opt and built from coordinates alone. Neither ascent is proved to attain $\max_\pi w(\pi)$, so every accuracy figure below is a floor on what this family reaches.  


\subsection{Results on the Multidimensional Benchmark} \label{subsec:frontier_nd}

At an ascent budget of 200 (Table~\ref{tab:frontier_nd}) the bound reaches 0.111\% MAPE against GART 2.0's 0.618\%, a factor of 5.56, at 0.709 times the cost, and it is the more accurate of the two on 82.9\% of instances. It first overtakes GART 2.0 at a budget of 100, where it costs 0.400 times as much.

The bound is both cheaper and more accurate than GART 2.0 in every dimension group except $d\in\{2,3\}$, and its lead is widest where the estimator was never trained. At $d\in\{2,3\}$ matching GART 2.0's accuracy costs the bound 1.27 times as much.

That advantage is an artifact of instance size. Of the 16{,}846 scored instances 10{,}569, or 62.7\%, carry at most 100 nodes, where the relaxation is nearly exact and nearly free, so a corpus mean over that mix describes small instances more than it describes the estimator. We therefore price the two methods on 20 instances per (dimension, size band) cell, matched on size across dimensions, and time them the same way as Appendix Table~\ref{tab:frontier_tsplib}. The bound's cost is far more load-sensitive than GART 2.0's. Accuracy in each cell is measured on every instance both methods score, between 108 and 2{,}209 of them. At $n\ge200$ no budget is both cheaper and more accurate than GART 2.0 in any dimension it was trained on. Only at $d=100$, the dimension withheld from training, does the bound beat it on both counts, and there GART 2.0's own accuracy degrades to 0.824\% against 0.0622\% at $d=50$.

In the $n\in[600,1000]$ band the highest budget at which GART 2.0 is still both cheaper and more accurate is $k=500$ in the plane and $k=200$ at $d=3$ and $d=4$. The bound reads 0.784\% MAPE at 246~ms for $k=500$ at $d=2$, 0.450\% at 144~ms for $k=200$ at $d=3$, and 0.291\% at 86.2~ms for the same budget at $d=4$. Outright reversal, with GART 2.0 cheaper and more accurate across the budgets the row lists, arrives only in the plane and at $d=3$, from roughly 200 nodes upward.

Roughly one instance in seven exhausts the largest budget we ran, so at the top of the size range the reported accuracy is a floor. Read any cell whose verdict turns on a few milliseconds as a trade-off: the ascent stops early when its 1-tree is already a tour, or when its bound reaches the cost of the tour it built for itself, so a larger budget can occasionally time a fraction of a millisecond cheaper than a smaller one. Table~\ref{tab:sizestrat} measures a constant factor between the two families, in the estimator's favor at $n\ge200$ and in the bound's below it. That constant is not a complexity argument, and we do not extrapolate it past $n=1000$, the largest size this corpus carries.

\subsection{Complexity of the Estimator Families} \label{subsec:frontier_complexity}

The 1-tree cannot use the Delaunay shortcut the MST family relies on in the plane, where a minimum spanning tree needs only triangulation edges. The node potentials leave costs that are no longer Euclidean distances, so after the first iteration the perturbed minimum spanning tree is not a subgraph of the triangulation. At 144 potential vectors drawn from real ascent runs the 1-tree restricted to Delaunay edges is heavier than the unrestricted one at 141 of them: the bound sits a complexity class above the MST family, not a constant factor above it.

In the plane GART 2.0 is $\Theta(n\log n)$ and the bound is $\Theta(n^{2}d+k n^{2})$, and the measured \emph{cost exponent}, the power of $n$ that per-instance cost grows with, agrees with both: 0.975 for GART 2.0 above a thousand nodes against 2.08--2.13 for the bound over the same window. GART 2.0 costs a flat 1.99 to 2.34 times the MST-ratio family across four orders of magnitude in $n$, while the 1-tree at a fixed budget costs 7.70 times GART 2.0 at a thousand nodes and 211 times at sixteen thousand (Figure~\ref{fig:costscale}).

That separation holds only in the plane. On the multidimensional benchmark GART 2.0's own measured cost exponent in $n$ is 2.02 at $d\in[4,10]$ and 1.82 at $d\in[15,50]$, against 1.78--1.89 for the bound, because from $d=4$ upward the MST construction has to compute every pairwise distance. At $d\in\{2,3\}$ that exponent is 0.556. Both families are quadratic on that benchmark, and the constant factor runs in the bound's favor. The bound is $\Theta(kn^{2})$ in every dimension, so the cost of matching a fixed accuracy grows quadratically while the estimator's cost carries no accuracy term.

\subsection{Operating Regime} \label{subsec:frontier_verdict}

In the plane GART 2.0 is cheaper than a 1-tree bound above roughly 400 nodes and more expensive below roughly 150 (Appendix Table~\ref{tab:frontier_tsplib}). Its accuracy sits between the bound's and the constant-ratio family's: on the full 78-instance EUC\_2D set it is 0.997 percentage points better than the MST-ratio family and 1.35 points worse than the bound run to convergence.

On the aggregate of every benchmark here the bound is the better instrument, because that aggregate is dominated by small instances. Read at fixed size instead (Table~\ref{tab:sizestrat}), the ordering turns over. In the two larger size bands GART 2.0 is cheaper and more accurate than the bound from an ascent budget of 25 upward in the plane, and over the middle budgets at $d=3$.

Below roughly 200 nodes, prefer the known lower bound at any dimension (Appendix Table~\ref{tab:frontier_2d}). It is cheaper and more accurate, it needs no training corpus, and it tells the caller how wrong it can be. Above that size prefer the estimator at any dimension it was trained on, in the plane and at $d=3$ where the ordering reverses. Each ascent iteration costs the bound $\Theta(n^{2})$ at a fixed accuracy in every dimension, while the estimator's planar cost stays subquadratic. At $d=100$, outside the training grid, prefer the bound at every size. Large $n$ does not rescue the estimator there, and the reason is its own degradation off the grid, not the bound's strength. Given only a distance matrix, prefer the bound at any size (Appendix~\ref{subsec:frontier_other}). Where inputs are known to be independent, uniformly distributed on a known region and an error in the high single digits of percent is acceptable, the classical closed forms remain the cheapest instrument by an order of magnitude over GART 2.0, with no MST to build. In the last-mile and partitioning setting of Section~\ref{sec:intro}, large instances are re-evaluated many times under changing input: an exact solve is out of reach, a quadratic bound is already expensive, and GART 2.0's cost stays sub-quadratic. 

\section{Application: Non-Euclidean Estimation via MDS Embedding} \label{sec:application}

We evaluate a hybrid pipeline that computes MST features from the original matrix and geometric features from a multidimensional-scaling (MDS) embedding. TSPLIB95 contains 33 non-EUC\_2D instances: 4 CEIL\_2D, 2 ATT, 10 GEO, and 17 EXPLICIT. An exhaustive triangle-inequality scan identifies ten EXPLICIT matrices with structural violations (worst direct-edge ratio at least 1.2), which are outside every metric-dependent aggregate in this paper: \texttt{brg180}, \texttt{brazil58}, \texttt{gr120}, \texttt{gr48}, \texttt{gr24}, \texttt{gr21}, \texttt{bays29}, \texttt{hk48}, \texttt{dantzig42}, and \texttt{gr17}. That leaves 23 instances, seven of them EXPLICIT, and three of those (\texttt{swiss42}, \texttt{pa561}, and \texttt{fri26}) carry mild rounding-level violations, so we restrict our conclusions to this screened set. The paired reference is a constant multiple of the original-distance MST, a fixed $\alpha=1.14$ close to the MAPE-minimizing constant $1.13$ over the full 111-instance TSPLIB set. The MAPE-minimizing constant over these 23 instances is $1.17$ and reaches 7.55\%, the floor any constant multiplier faces on this metric.

\subsection{MDS Preprocessing}

CEIL\_2D cases use their native coordinates. For ATT, GEO, and EXPLICIT cases, classical MDS gives an approximate Euclidean embedding of the distance matrix \citep{torgerson1952mds,gower1966some,borg2005mds,cox2000mds}. We select the smallest embedding dimension $\kappa$ retaining at least 99.9\% of positive eigenvalue mass, subject to $\kappa\le100$, and pass $\kappa$ as the model's \texttt{dimension} feature. The cap prevents five of 19 MDS cases from reaching 99.9\%; hence this criterion does not imply distance preservation.

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}

The GART 2.0 framework, trained on synthetic data, is competitive on real-world problems. Exposing the model to more relevant training data can move it from a prototype to a viable product.

The estimator's advantage is scale. The known lower bound is cheaper and more accurate on the aggregate of all four benchmarks, and on TSPLIB EUC\_2D once a single training-split constant scales it. On the non-Euclidean instances it reads directly, the bound obtains 1.27\% MAPE at an ascent budget of 100 against GART 2.0's 3.81\%. Above a few hundred nodes, however, no ascent budget is both cheaper and more accurate than GART 2.0 at any dimension it was trained on. In the plane the measured cost exponents put the two families in different complexity classes (Section~\ref{sec:frontier}). Below a few hundred nodes, off the training grid in dimension, or given only a distance matrix, prefer the bound. The model weights, the scored benchmark outputs, and the scripts that regenerate every table and figure are released (Appendix~\ref{app:code}).

On the 2D diverse benchmark GART 2.0's MAPE runs from 1.47\% on the isotropic class to 10.8\% on the near-collinear Line Noise class across generator classes, and from 1.64\% at $n\in[501,1000]$ to 4.07\% at $n\in[11,50]$ across size bands. Section~\ref{subsec:coverage} traces the Line Noise error to a training-coverage gap and a feature gap at once. We tested two repairs against gates fixed before either candidate was scored, rejected both, and left the released model unchanged. Withholding a whole dimension or the large-$n$ range from training costs a fraction of a MAPE point and appears as a systematic offset, not as scatter. The estimator degrades predictably off its training grid, and we demonstrate no correction for that offset. The labels are not uniformly proven optima. Some are proven optimal by a 1-tree bound that closes on the tour and some by exact dynamic programming (Appendix~\ref{app:labels}), and the remainder carry an upper bound.

Can a learned estimator understand the relationship between the MST and the TSP to create an efficient tour-length estimator?

\section{Training Data Generation} \label{app:training}



\subsection{Training Corpus}

The uniform class draws coordinates from $\mathcal{U}(0, G)$ where $G$ is the grid size. The normal class draws from $\mathcal{N}(G/2, G/6)$ and clips to $[0, G]$. The clustered class places $\max(2, \lfloor \sqrt{n}/4 \rfloor)$ centers uniformly, assigns each point to a center uniformly at random, draws it from a normal of standard deviation $0.05\,G$ about that center, and clips to $[0, G]$. A correlated axis adds $\mathcal{N}(0, G/10)$ noise to the first non-correlated axis already drawn and clips to $[0, G]$; an axis drawn before any non-correlated one falls back to a uniform draw.



The $106{,}272$ solved instances are partitioned into training ($69{,}768$, $65.7\%$), validation ($19{,}584$, $18.4\%$), and test ($16{,}920$, $15.9\%$) using a stratified sample on $(d, n, \text{grid size})$. $d = 100$ instances bypass this rule and are locked to the test partition, which lifts the corpus-level test share to the $15.9\%$ above. Grid size $G \in \{100, 1{,}000, 10{,}000\}$ is sampled uniformly within each cell.

\subsection{2D Benchmark Generator Design} \label{app:gen2d}

Four classes (isotropic, biased, geometric, clustered) follow conventions from the TSP-instance-generation literature, including the variants \citet{cavdar2015distribution} reports for the distribution-free baseline. The fifth class, Line Noise, samples $y = mx + b + \epsilon$ with small $\epsilon$ and clips out-of-range coordinates to the grid boundary instead of resampling them. Its slope reaches $\pm5$ while $x$ is drawn across the full grid width, so most points fall outside the box and are pinned to a face. The realized geometry is a union of two to four thin segments with dense collinear masses along the boundary. At $n\ge200$ a median 59.9\% of points lie exactly on a face, and $\alpha$ tracks that fraction closely (Spearman 0.86, rising monotonically from 1.18 to 1.43 across its quartiles) with no relationship to the transverse noise width. The \texttt{grid} member of the Geometric Struct.\ class draws a jittered square lattice, a pattern the four independent per-axis draws of Section~\ref{subsec:dataset} cannot produce; the training corpus contains no lattice instance.

\section{Label Validation and Verification} \label{app:labels}

We recompute the length of every stored tour on its own released coordinates, over all $106{,}272$ instances. The 184 instances (0.173\%) whose stored cost their own tour does not reproduce to within rounding are not scored. 74 of the 184 fall in the multidimensional test partition, so it is scored on the remaining $16{,}846$ instances throughout.

If $B$ is a proven lower bound on an instance's optimum and $L$ is its stored label, $B>L$ refutes the label outright. At $n\le10$ the label is the exact Held--Karp dynamic-programming optimum. Above that size the label is the float64 length of the retained tour, an upper bound that is exact wherever that tour is optimal; a 1-tree bound closes on the tour and proves it optimal after the fact for $2{,}182$ instances. The 2D benchmark's labels are the better of the stored tour and a float-metric 2-opt descent from it, because a solver that minimizes a rounded metric can select a tour the float64 metric beats. TSPLIB is scored on its published optima, with one documented exception: \texttt{linhp318} declares a fixed-edge Hamiltonian-path problem on \texttt{lin318}'s coordinates, so its published 41{,}345 is a path optimum no tour on those coordinates can attain. Every estimator and the bound are scored there against the tour optimum on those coordinates, $42{,}029$, \texttt{lin318}'s published value.

Across the 145{,}013 bound checks in the released artifacts there are zero violations of $B\le L$, the worst relative excess being $5.8\times10^{-15}$. On TSPLIB the check builds the bound in TSPLIB's own integer metric, which needs the dense distance matrix, and for seven instances that matrix does not fit in memory; five of the seven are inside the scored EUC\_2D set.

\section{Benchmark-Model Implementation} \label{app:bench_details}

We attribute the tour-length estimators of Daganzo \citep{daganzo1984a}, Chien \citep{chien1992operational}, and Kwon--Golden--Wasil \citep{kwon1995tsp}, surveyed in \citet{figliozzi2009planning}. They belong to the same class of estimator, and we score BHH and \c{C}avdar--Sokol as its representatives.

\subsection{BHH}

BHH assumes points drawn independently from a density $f$ of bounded support: $L_n/n^{(d-1)/d}\to\beta_d\int f(x)^{(d-1)/d}\,dx$ \citep{bhh1959}. For $f$ uniform on a region of measure $\mathcal{V}$ the integral reduces to $\mathcal{V}^{1/d}$, giving $\beta_d n^{(d-1)/d}\mathcal{V}^{1/d}$. The convex hull of a realized sample is a different, downward-biased statistic that converges to $\mathcal{V}$ only as $n$ grows. Our synthetic generators draw on $[0,G]^d$, so $\mathcal{V}=G^d$ is exact, and we report that form on the matched domain. We use $\beta_2=0.7124\pm0.0002$ \citep{johnson1996asymptotic} and $\beta_3=0.6979\pm0.0002$ \citep{percus1996finite}. For $d\ge4$ no comparably pinned estimate exists, so we substitute the large-$d$ asymptotic $\sqrt{d/(2\pi e)}$ \citep{bertsimas1990asymptotic}, a form the same source proves for the minimum-spanning-tree and minimum-matching constants and only conjectures for the TSP constant. That substitution, together with $n\le1000$ at $d\ge4$ falling short of the asymptotic regime, limits how far the $d\ge4$ rows test the theorem itself.

\subsection{\c{C}avdar--Sokol}

We rotate each instance into its minimum-area enclosing rectangle by scanning convex-hull edges \citep{freeman1975determining}, because \c{C}avdar--Sokol assumes an axis-aligned rectangle. $A$ is the area of that rectangle, and $\bar c_j$ and $\mathrm{cstdev}_j$ are the mean and standard deviation of the absolute distances from the nodes to its midpoint line on axis $j$, not to the sample mean. We divide the raw estimate by the finite-$n$ correction $0.9325e^{0.00005298n}-0.2972e^{-0.01452n}$, Eq.~(21) of \citet{cavdar2014dissertation}, fitted over $n\in\{100,125,\dots,975\}$ at $R^2=0.987$, and we bound the correction to that range. On this benchmark 1{,}320 of the 2{,}580 2D instances have $n<100$, and we evaluate the ratio at the $n=100$ endpoint for them instead of extrapolating below it. Above $n=975$ we apply no correction, leaving a 1.84\% step at the upper boundary where the fitted ratio reads 0.982. The source regression targets Lin--Kernighan tour lengths, not optima.

\subsection{Constant-Ratio References}

The $\alpha=1$ row returns $L_{\mathrm{MST}}$. The asymptotic row uses $\beta_{\mathrm{TSP}}/\beta_{\mathrm{MST}}$ for uniform planar points. The numerator is well established at $0.7124$; the denominator is not. We quote $0.6331$ following \citet{cortinaborja2000mst}, a simulation estimate, and published estimates of the planar Euclidean MST constant span a range wider than its printed precision, so the ratio $1.1253$ is approximate. The calibrated rows take the mean of $\alpha$ within each dimension, and within each dimension--size cell, over the training split only. At $d=100$, which no training row covers, the table extrapolates from $d=50$.

\subsection{Hilbert Sort}

The custom Hilbert sort orders nodes by a $d$-dimensional Hilbert index \citep{hilbert1891} on per-axis normalized coordinates.





\subsection{Convex-Hull Cap}

The convex-hull plug-in uses the hull only for $d\le3$; above that we substitute the coordinate bounding box. QHull's output size grows as $n^{\lfloor d/2\rfloor}$, the blow-up of Section~\ref{sec:intro}. At $d=8$ with $n=1000$ a single hull dominates the entire benchmark's runtime.
